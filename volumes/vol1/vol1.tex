\begin{volumeintro}
\lettrine[lines=3]{B}{efore we} can understand how arrays store elements, we must first understand how computers represent information itself. Every piece of data---numbers, text, images, instructions---exists as patterns of bits, and every bit begins its life as a voltage. This volume follows that single thread: from a voltage difference across a transistor, through binary encoding, to the numbers, characters, and bit-level structures that everything else in this work is built from.

\begin{quote}
\textit{``The choice of representation is often more important than the choice of algorithm.''}

\hfill--- \textsc{Donald E. Knuth}
\end{quote}
\end{volumeintro}

\chapter{Electric Charge and Matter}
\label{ch:electric-charge}

Atoms and subatomic particles: protons, neutrons, and electrons; electric
charge as a fundamental property of matter; the coulomb as the unit of
charge; conservation of charge; positive and negative charge and why matter
is usually electrically neutral; charging by friction, conduction, and
induction; Coulomb's law: force between charges; the elementary charge of a
single electron; why understanding charge is the starting point for
understanding electricity.

\chapter{Electric Current}
\label{ch:electric-current}

Flow of charge through a conductor; conventional current direction versus
actual electron flow; the ampere as the unit of current (one coulomb per
second); direct current (DC) versus alternating current (AC); current in
everyday devices; why electrons drift slowly even though electrical effects
propagate near the speed of light; measuring current with an ammeter;
what happens at the atomic level when current flows through a metal.

\chapter{Voltage and Electric Potential}
\label{ch:voltage}

Electric potential as stored energy per unit charge; potential difference
(voltage) as the driving force for current; the volt as the unit of voltage;
batteries and voltage sources as pumps for charge; electromotive force (EMF);
ground and the reference potential; measuring voltage with a voltmeter;
why voltage is always a relative measurement between two points; the
analogy between voltage and water pressure.

\chapter{Resistance and Ohm's Law}
\label{ch:resistance-ohms-law}

Opposition to current flow; resistance and the ohm; Ohm's law (V = IR) and
its experimental basis; resistivity and conductivity as material properties;
how resistance depends on a conductor's material, length, cross-sectional
area, and temperature; power dissipation: P = IV = I\textsuperscript{2}R =
V\textsuperscript{2}/R; heat generation in resistors; linear versus
non-linear resistive elements; why resistance matters for digital circuits:
pull-up resistors, current limiting, signal termination.

\chapter{Basic Circuit Laws and Analysis}
\label{ch:circuit-laws}

Closed circuits, open circuits, and short circuits; Kirchhoff's voltage law
(KVL): the sum of voltages around any closed loop equals zero; Kirchhoff's
current law (KCL): the sum of currents at any node equals zero; series
circuits and the voltage divider rule; parallel circuits and the current
divider rule; combining resistances in series and parallel; simple circuit
analysis: finding unknown voltages and currents; why these laws are the
foundation for analysing every circuit in this volume.

\chapter{Capacitors: Storing Electric Charge}
\label{ch:capacitors}

Storing energy in an electric field; the capacitor as two conductors separated
by an insulator; capacitance and the farad; the parallel plate capacitor;
charging and discharging dynamics; the RC time constant (\(\tau = RC\));
capacitors in series and parallel; energy stored in a capacitor
(\(E = \tfrac{1}{2}CV^2\)); the role of capacitors in real digital circuits:
power supply filtering, signal bypassing, decoupling each IC's power pin from
noise on the supply rail; why every chip datasheet specifies decoupling
capacitors.

\chapter{Inductors and Magnetic Fields}
\label{ch:inductors}

Magnetic fields around current-carrying conductors; inductance and the henry;
Faraday's law of induction: a changing magnetic field induces a voltage;
the RL time constant; energy stored in a magnetic field (\(E = \tfrac{1}{2}LI^2\));
inductors in series and parallel; where inductors appear in practice: switching
power supplies, RF filters, EMI suppression; inductive kickback and why it
damages transistors without protection diodes.

\chapter{Analog Signals and Waveforms}
\label{ch:analog-signals}

Continuous-valued signals; voltage as a function of time; sinusoidal waves:
frequency, period, amplitude, and phase; the decibel scale for expressing
signal ratios; other waveform shapes (square, triangle, sawtooth, pulse);
superposition: multiple signals added together; noise as an unwanted signal;
bandwidth as the range of frequencies a system can faithfully carry;
why analog representation of information is inherently fragile: noise
accumulates and cannot be separated from the signal.

\chapter{Electronic Components: A Survey}

\chapter{Diodes: One-Way Current Flow}
\label{ch:diodes}

The diode as a one-directional valve; forward bias: current flows; reverse

\chapter{Printed Circuit Boards}
\label{ch:pcbs}

What a PCB is: a mechanical substrate that also serves as the wiring harness;

\chapter{Component Arrays on PCBs}

Why components are often grouped into arrays; resistor networks (SIP and

\chapter{Through-Hole and Surface-Mount Components}
\label{ch:component-packages}

Through-hole technology (THT): component leads passing through drilled board

much higher component density; passive SMT sizes: 0402, 0603, 0805, 1206

the assembly process: solder paste, pick-and-place machines, reflow ovens.

\chapter{Integrated Circuits: From Discrete to Monolithic}
\label{ch:ics-overview}

Why combining many transistors on a single silicon die changed computing;

the wire bonds, and the package; how millions then billions of transistors
fit on a fingernail-sized piece of silicon; reading an IC datasheet: pinout
diagram, absolute maximum ratings, recommended operating conditions,
electrical characteristics, timing diagrams; the difference between a
simple logic gate IC, a microcontroller, a CPU, and a memory chip;
why a CPU and a DRAM chip look the same from the outside but do
fundamentally different things.

\chapter{Power Supply and Voltage Regulation}
\label{ch:power-supply}

Power rails on a PCB and why digital systems need tightly regulated voltages;
typical supply voltages and their historical progression: 5V TTL era,
3.3V, 1.8V, 1.2V, 1.0V, sub-1V; why lower supply voltage reduces power
dissipation quadratically; linear voltage regulators: dropout voltage,
power dissipation as heat, efficiency; switching regulators (buck, boost,
buck-boost): higher efficiency, switching noise tradeoff; decoupling
capacitors: suppressing noise on the supply rail at each IC; power
sequencing: some chips must be powered in a specific order; how power
supply quality directly affects digital signal reliability.

\chapter{Signal Integrity and Interconnects}
\label{ch:signal-integrity-pcb}

Why wires are not ideal at high frequencies; transmission line effects:
traces have distributed inductance and capacitance; impedance matching and
why mismatches cause reflections; crosstalk between adjacent traces through
electric and magnetic coupling; differential pairs: two traces carrying
complementary signals, immune to common-mode noise; ground planes as the
return path for every signal; ground bounce: the inductance of the ground
connection causes brief voltage spikes; why board layout is not just
mechanical but an electrical engineering problem; how signal integrity
concerns become severe above roughly 100\,MHz.

\chapter{Semiconductors: Between Conductor and Insulator}
\label{ch:semiconductors-intro}

Conductors, insulators, and semiconductors as a spectrum of electrical
conductivity; why silicon is the dominant semiconductor: abundance, stable
oxide, mature fabrication; the silicon crystal lattice and covalent bonding
of four valence electrons; intrinsic semiconductors: thermal generation of
electron-hole pairs; the concept of holes as positive charge carriers that
move in the direction opposite to electrons; why temperature dramatically
increases semiconductor conductivity (unlike metals); a brief tour of other
semiconductors: germanium (first transistors), gallium arsenide (high
frequency), silicon carbide (high power), gallium nitride (LEDs, power).

\chapter{Doping: Engineering Conductivity}
\label{ch:doping}

Why pure silicon is not useful on its own; extrinsic semiconductors created
by intentionally adding impurities; n-type doping: donor atoms (phosphorus,
arsenic, antimony) with five valence electrons contribute one free electron
each; p-type doping: acceptor atoms (boron, aluminium, gallium) with three
valence electrons create holes; majority carriers and minority carriers;
how doping concentration determines resistivity across many orders of
magnitude; the depletion approximation; why precise doping control is the
central challenge of semiconductor manufacturing.

\chapter{The P-N Junction}
\label{ch:pn-junction}

Bringing n-type and p-type silicon into contact; diffusion of carriers across
the boundary; the depletion region: a zone swept clear of mobile carriers;
the built-in electric field opposing further diffusion; contact potential
(built-in voltage); forward bias: an external voltage reduces the depletion
region and allows current to flow exponentially; reverse bias: an external
voltage widens the depletion region, current is nearly zero; the ideal diode
equation; avalanche and Zener breakdown at high reverse voltage; why the
p-n junction is the foundational non-linear element from which all active
devices are built.

\chapter{The Bipolar Junction Transistor}
\label{ch:bjt}

Two back-to-back p-n junctions forming a three-layer device: NPN (n-p-n) and
PNP (p-n-p) structures; the three terminals: base, collector, and emitter;
minority carrier injection across the thin base region; current gain
(\(\beta\)): collector current as a multiple of base current; the three
regions of BJT operation: cutoff (base-emitter junction reverse biased —
transistor off), active (base-emitter forward biased, base-collector reverse
biased — amplifier region), saturation (both junctions forward biased —
transistor fully on); using the BJT as a switch: driving it between cutoff
and saturation; speed limitations: stored minority carriers slow switching;
historical importance: the first transistors (1947, Bell Labs) and why they
replaced vacuum tubes.

\chapter{The MOSFET: Field-Effect Switching}
\label{ch:mosfet}

The metal-oxide-semiconductor field-effect transistor; the four terminals:
gate, source, drain, and body; the gate oxide (silicon dioxide) as an
insulator: no DC current flows into the gate; the threshold voltage
V\textsubscript{th}: the minimum gate-to-source voltage that creates a
conductive channel; n-channel MOSFET (NMOS): a positive gate voltage
attracts electrons and creates an n-type channel between source and drain;
p-channel MOSFET (PMOS): a negative gate voltage creates a p-type channel;
enhancement-mode versus depletion-mode; the MOSFET I-V characteristics in
cutoff, linear (triode), and saturation regions; why the MOSFET dominates
digital logic: voltage-controlled (nearly zero gate current), easily scaled
to smaller sizes, and naturally suited to CMOS pairing.

\chapter{Transistors as Binary Switches}
\label{ch:transistors-as-switches}

Using a MOSFET in only two of its operating regions: cutoff (off, gate below
V\textsubscript{th}) and linear (on, gate well above V\textsubscript{th});
on-resistance R\textsubscript{DS(on)}: the channel acts like a small resistor
when fully on; switching time: how long it takes to charge/discharge the gate
capacitance and transition between states; the trade-off between on-resistance
and switching speed as transistor size scales; drive strength: why some logic
outputs can source/sink more current and switch faster; the MOSFET as the
physical realisation of a single bit's worth of state control.

\chapter{CMOS Technology}
\label{ch:cmos-intro}

Complementary MOS: pairing NMOS and PMOS transistors; the CMOS inverter as
the foundational building block: a PMOS pull-up and an NMOS pull-down
connected drain-to-drain; when input is low, PMOS conducts, output is pulled
high; when input is high, NMOS conducts, output is pulled low; why one
transistor is always off in steady state: zero static power dissipation;
dynamic power: energy consumed charging and discharging capacitances during
switching, proportional to CV\textsuperscript{2}f; why lower supply voltage
reduces power quadratically; CMOS versus earlier logic families (TTL, ECL,
NMOS-only) and why CMOS won; CMOS NAND and NOR gate topology.

\chapter{Transistor Scaling and Moore's Law}
\label{ch:transistor-scaling}

Gordon Moore's 1965 observation: transistor count on a chip doubles roughly
every two years; feature size (process node): the characteristic dimension
of transistors in nanometres; how shrinking transistors increases density,
speed, and reduces power simultaneously; Dennard scaling: as transistors
shrink, voltage and power density stay constant — and why this relationship
broke down around 2006; the power wall: heat dissipation limiting further
clock frequency increases; short-channel effects at small geometries:
drain-induced barrier lowering (DIBL), threshold voltage variation, gate
oxide tunnelling leakage; how the industry responded: multi-gate transistors
(FinFET), gate-all-around (GAA, nanosheet), stacked dies; Moore's law
today — still alive in transistor density, but no longer in the simple
doubling form Moore observed.

\chapter{Analog Versus Digital: The Fundamental Divide}
\label{ch:analog-vs-digital}

Continuous-valued versus discrete-valued representation; why analog signals
carry theoretically infinite information but accumulate noise without bound;
quantisation: mapping a continuous range to a finite set of levels;
the key insight: a discrete signal does not need to be precise, only
unambiguous; regeneration — restoring a degraded digital signal to a clean
value at every logic gate — versus an analog signal where every copy
degrades further; why the shift to digital representation in the 20th
century was not obvious and what it cost in terms of the information
discarded.

\chapter{Logic Levels and Voltage Standards}
\label{ch:logic-levels}

Defining logic 0 and logic 1 as voltage ranges rather than exact values;
input thresholds V\textsubscript{IL} (maximum voltage still read as 0) and
V\textsubscript{IH} (minimum voltage read as 1); output guarantees
V\textsubscript{OL} (maximum output voltage for logic 0) and
V\textsubscript{OH} (minimum output voltage for logic 1); noise margin:
the difference between what an output guarantees and what an input requires,
representing the system's tolerance for distortion; common logic families
and their voltage levels: 5V TTL, 5V CMOS, 3.3V LVCMOS, 2.5V, 1.8V,
1.2V, sub-1V; level-shifting between families with different voltage
standards; why the industry has moved progressively to lower voltages.

\chapter{Why Binary? Comparing Representation Bases}
\label{ch:why-binary}

Could a transistor be designed to have three stable voltage states instead
of two? Ternary logic: historical attempts and physical obstacles;
the signal-to-noise argument: with N levels the noise margin shrinks as
the supply voltage divided by N, while circuit complexity grows; why two
levels maximise the noise margin for a given supply voltage and threshold
spread; decimal computers: early machines (ENIAC used decimal) and why
they were abandoned; the alignment between two-state physics and Boolean
algebra: a system with exactly two values is the natural domain of the
logic Shannon formalised; why binary is not an arbitrary choice but a
consequence of physics and mathematics meeting.

\chapter{Noise, Interference, and the Reliability of Binary}
\label{ch:noise-binary}

Sources of noise in digital systems: thermal (Johnson-Nyquist) noise,
shot noise, power-supply noise, ground bounce, crosstalk between traces,
electromagnetic interference (EMI) from external sources; why binary levels
survive noise that would corrupt an analog signal of the same amplitude;
regenerative logic: every gate restores its output to a full rail voltage,
preventing error accumulation; error accumulation in long analog chains
versus error-free propagation in digital chains of arbitrary length;
the role of noise margins in practical system design: why they must never
be zero, and what happens when marginal designs encounter real-world
conditions.

\chapter{Clock Signals and Timing}
\label{ch:clocks-timing}

The clock as the heartbeat of a synchronous digital system; crystal
oscillators: mechanical resonance of quartz as an extremely stable frequency
reference; clock frequency and period; duty cycle; rising and falling edges
as the events that trigger state changes; edge-triggered versus
level-triggered logic; setup time (t\textsubscript{su}): data must be
stable before the clock edge; hold time (t\textsubscript{h}): data must
remain stable after the clock edge; why violating setup or hold time
causes metastability; clock distribution across a chip or board; clock
skew: the difference in arrival time at different destinations, and why
it limits maximum frequency; the relationship between clock period and
the maximum combinational path delay — a foundation for the pipeline
discussion in Volume II.

\chapter{From Transistor Switch to Stored Bit}
\label{ch:switch-to-bit}

Closing the loop from Stage 2 to here: a MOSFET gate voltage above
V\textsubscript{th} turns the channel on, pulling the drain to near ground —
that output voltage is logic 0; a gate voltage below V\textsubscript{th}
leaves the channel off, and a pull-up element holds the output near the
supply voltage — that is logic 1; the SR latch: two cross-coupled NOR
gates that retain a state even after the input is removed; the D latch
and D flip-flop: storing an arbitrary bit value on a clock edge; the
static RAM (SRAM) cell: six transistors (four forming two inverters
cross-coupled, two for access) holding one bit indefinitely; why this
is the physical realisation of the abstract concept of ``a bit in memory'';
the connection to everything that follows — all of the encodings in
the rest of this volume exist as patterns of such stored bits.

How the compiler inserts padding bytes between fields to satisfy each

and an unaligned load; practical patterns for high-throughput array code.
